Real estate prospecting letters are short, personal pieces of mail that introduce you to a homeowner and ask for one small next step. They are slow compared with a call and easy to ignore, so the ones that work are specific, honest and sent to the right list. This guide covers what to put in real estate prospecting letters, three templates you can adapt for sellers, expired listings and just sold homes, and the rules to check before you mail.

What Are Real Estate Prospecting Letters?

Real estate prospecting letters are one-to-one messages sent by mail to people who may list or buy soon. They differ from a postcard because they read like a note from a person, with a name, a reason for writing and a single ask. They work best as part of a steady plan such as real estate farming, where the same homeowners see you more than once.

Do not expect one letter to produce a listing. A letter earns a second look, and the follow-up earns the conversation. Treat each batch as one touch in a sequence you can repeat, and place it in your real estate marketing plan alongside your other channels.

What Should Real Estate Prospecting Letters Include?

Whatever the type, six parts do the work. These come from common guidance on writing expired listing and seller letters, and they apply to the other types too:

  1. The reader’s name. Address a person, not “Dear Homeowner,” when you can.
  2. The reason you are writing. Say it in the first two lines.
  3. One piece of local proof. A recent sale on their street is stronger than a claim that you are an expert.
  4. A note of empathy where it fits. For a listing that did not sell, acknowledge that it is frustrating.
  5. One difference. Say one thing you do that others do not, such as how you market a home.
  6. One clear ask. A short call, a home value report or a coffee, not three options.

Keep the letter to one page, and add your brokerage name and a way to reach you in the footer. A handwritten envelope or a short handwritten line at the end helps the piece look personal. The table shows how the main types differ.

Real Estate Prospecting Letters Templates You Can Adapt

Replace the items in brackets and keep each of these real estate letters to one page. They are starting points, not scripts to send unchanged.

Seller letter

Dear [Name],
I work with homeowners on [Street or neighborhood], and I noticed that [a nearby home] recently sold. People often ask me what their own homes would sell for now, so I put together a short report for your address. I can send it to you at no cost and with no obligation. If you would like it, call me at [number] or reply to this letter.
[Your name], [Brokerage]

Expired listing letter

Dear [Name],
I saw that your home at [address] is no longer on the market. A listing that ends without a sale is frustrating after all the work you put in. I would like to share what I see in the local market and a plan for [what you would change, such as pricing, photos or timing]. If you would like to talk, I can meet for 20 minutes at a time that suits you.
[Your name], [Brokerage]

Just sold letter

Dear [Name],
I recently helped a neighbor sell their home on [street], and I wanted you to hear it from me first. Buyers in [area] are looking for [a fact you can support]. If you are curious what that means for your home, I would be glad to send a free estimate.
[Your name], [Brokerage]

What Rules Apply to Real Estate Prospecting Letters?

Expired listing letters need extra care. NAR’s Code of Ethics and Standards of Practice bar practices inconsistent with another REALTOR®’s exclusive agreement. Standard of Practice 16-4 says you may not solicit a listing currently listed exclusively with another broker, and describes a narrow route if the listing broker refuses to say when the listing expires. In short, mail only to owners whose listing has actually expired, and check your local board’s rules too, because some boards add limits on how expired listing data can be used.

A letter is advertising. Fair housing rules apply, as set out in 24 CFR 100.75. Choose recipients by market data such as street, sales history or listing status, never by who you think lives there, and keep the wording neutral.

Use brokerage details correctly. Include your brokerage name and check your state’s advertising rules on what must appear. Ask your broker to review a template once, and then reuse it.

Calls are different from mail. A letter is not a phone call. If your letter says you will call, check the National Do Not Call Registry before you dial, and read our guide to TCPA compliance for real estate agents first.

How Often Should You Send Real Estate Letters?

Match the rhythm to the type. These are our suggestions, so adjust them to your list and budget:

  • Seller letters: Send them to a farm on a regular schedule, such as every quarter, and change the proof each time.
  • Just sold letters: Send one batch after each sale, soon after closing, so the news is fresh.
  • Expired listing letters: Send one as soon as a listing has truly expired, and follow up once with a different message rather than the same letter again.

How Do You Send and Follow Up on Real Estate Prospecting Letters?

Pick a list and a date, mail the batch together, and plan the next touch before you send it. A simple order is the letter, then a postcard a few weeks later, then a call or a visit if the rules allow. Common Mistakes in Real Estate Prospecting Letters

  • Dear Homeowner. It tells the reader this is a mass mailing.
  • Writing about yourself. Lead with the reader and the local market.
  • Too long. A page is plenty.
  • Several asks. One request is easier to answer than three.
  • Mailing the wrong list. An expired letter to a home that is still listed can break the Code of Ethics.
  • No second touch. One letter is a start, not a campaign.

Final Thoughts

Good real estate prospecting letters are personal, local and short. Choose a clean list, write one page with one proof point and one ask, check the Code of Ethics and your local rules before you mail, and follow up on every reply the same day. Repeat the batch on a schedule, and measure which letter type brings calls.
